UN procurement from Russian suppliers, 2015–2025

Over 11 years, UN agencies paid at least $2.57 billion to suppliers registered in the Russian Federation. This page shows what that money bought, who paid it and how it moved year by year, and publishes the records underneath, so the charts can be rebuilt.

What this page adds is a shape you can read and a file you can download. Every chart carries its limits directly beneath it, and every number in it opens as a table.

Source: UN Annual Statistical Report on procurement, supplier-level data, taken 20 July 2026 and re-checked 12 September 2026 · The dataset and how to check it ↓

The big picture

That $2.57 billion is what the UN's own supplier-level data shows for large contracts, at or above $30,000. Smaller purchases are not published, so the real figure is higher. It is also contract value, not money spent: the UN reports these figures by contract amount, because many of its organizations cannot report actual expenditure (the UN's own explanation of this data).

What this data cannot say is why. The UN publishes who won a contract. It does not publish who competed for it — the bidders appear in this record no more than in any other it makes public. So nothing on this page can tell you whether Russian suppliers stopped bidding, whether their competitors grew stronger, whether UN organizations decided to buy elsewhere, or whether some mix of the three was at work. The figures show what happened, in what order and to whom. Why it happened is a separate inquiry, and it would have to be built on sources other than this dataset.

Three things stand out.

The data behind these charts

One flat file: 4,110 rows covering 2015–2025, one row per contract line. It is the same slice the charts on this page are built from.

The full logic behind this file — how it was built, what was measured and every caveat that follows from it — is set out in the README. What is below is the short version: open a drawer to read it here.

Before you count: four caveats that change the totals

Before you count. One row is not one contract — there are 4,110 rows but 3,418 contracts, and counting rows overstates contracts by about 20%; group by reference first. Supplier names are not standardised — 346 distinct spellings, which consolidate to roughly 296 groups, so a search by name will silently miss rows. In 2025 the source's country field broke — 10.4% of suppliers appear under more than one country, against 0.6–3.3% in every other year — so rows may be missing here or present wrongly. And every total is a floor, because only contracts at or above $30,000 are published at source. All four caveats in full, with the measurements behind them →

What is in the file: 4,110 rows, seven columns, one of them empty

It has seven columns, but one of them is empty of meaning: supplier_country reads Russian Federation in all 4,110 rows, because that is how the slice was defined. So there are six real dimensions — year (11), paying agency (24), supplier (346 spellings), category (47), value in US dollars, and contract reference (3,418).

Supplier names: 346 spellings, but fewer firms

The UN is not consistent in its own service labels. Where a supplier is not named, this file carries five different strings for it — NAME WITHHELD FOR PRIVACY REASONS, NAME WITHHELD FOR SECURITY REASONS, UNSPECIFIED SUPPLIER, INDIVIDUAL SUPPLIER and INDIVIDUAL SERVICE CONTRACTOR — together $183.3 million across 208 rows. Filter on one of them and you miss the other four.

346 is a count of spellings, not of firms. The same company appears in this file under several different strings, so the number of distinct suppliers is smaller — but by how much, nobody can say exactly. Consolidating the spellings gives roughly 296 groups, and that figure is a ceiling, not a tally: the true number of firms is no higher than 296 and otherwise unknown. Four of those 296 name nobody at all, so named suppliers number 292 at most.

The consolidation works in two layers. A strict mechanical rule merges only spellings that become identical once quotes, commas and legal forms are stripped, taking 346 down to 309. Thirty hand-written patterns then finish what the rule could not — a name truncated at about 34 characters, a different legal form, a different transliteration — taking 309 to 296. Those patterns were written for the large firms at the top of the table; the tail of 266 groups was never reviewed by hand, so small suppliers scattered across spellings are almost certainly still counted more than once. That is why the figure is a ceiling.

What “Russia” means here: registration, not ownership

“Russia” here means the country of registration, and nothing else. The field this slice is built on records where a supplier is registered — not who owns it, not where its money goes, not who controls it. Two consequences run in opposite directions, and both matter. A company registered in the Russian Federation may be foreign-owned. And a Russian-owned company registered anywhere else — Cyprus, the UAE, Kazakhstan, Turkey — does not appear in this file at all, however Russian it is in substance. Nothing here is evidence about ownership, and the total is not a measure of money reaching Russian owners.

What the file does not say: which office bought, and who for

The paying agency is named, but never the office. The organization column holds 24 values, and almost every one of them is a whole organization — UNDP, WFP, UNICEF, UN Secretariat. Three name a duty station or a regional commission (UNOG, UNOV, ESCAP, 23 rows between them); none is a country office. When a row says UNDP, there is no way to tell from this data whether the buyer was the country office in Kyiv, the one in Moscow, a regional hub or New York.

And the other end of the chain is missing too. There is no project, no programme, no mission and no recipient country in this file. So each row leaves both ends unknown: which part of the agency bought, and who the purchase was for. “UNDP · Russian Federation” means UNDP paid a supplier registered in Russia. It does not mean UNDP was buying for Russia. UNICEF bought $320.1m of pharmaceuticals, contraceptives and vaccines from Russia-registered suppliers over these years, and where any of it went is not recorded here. Any claim about destination has to come from a source outside this dataset.

The data carries a year, not a month. That is enough to say what happened in which year, and not enough to say that a payment followed any particular event. Any claim of sequence — that something happened because of something else — needs a source outside this file.

The 2025 break in the source’s country field

The 2025 break was checked at contract level, and it is the source's. A contract cannot have several supplier countries, yet in 2025 5,679 contracts do, against between zero and 46 in every earlier year. The same count on the raw downloads, before anything of ours was assembled, gives the same numbers, so our own handling did not cause it. In this file it reaches 18 of the 134 rows for 2025, $9.7m, and five of those name a supplier that does not look Russian. The full working, with the tables, is in the README.

How much of UN procurement is in this file

How much of UN procurement is in this file. Not all of it, and the gap is large enough to matter. The UN's own Annual Statistical Report counts every purchase its organizations make. The dashboard this file comes from carries only contracts at or above $30,000. Over 2015–2025 the dashboard therefore holds 19.2% less money than the report — $199.8 billion against $247.4 billion.

The UN's own annual totals, against what the dashboard carries

Two different things make up that gap, and only one of them is the threshold.

The $30,000 threshold accounts for the steady part. In eight of the eleven years the dashboard sits between 82% and 91% of the report — that missing tenth to fifth is the UN's small purchases, which are never published supplier by supplier. Leaving out 2021–2023, the shortfall is 15.2% and moves only between 9.5% and 17.8%.

2021–2023 are different, and the UN says why. In those years the shortfall jumps to 27.2%, because COVID-19 vaccine procurement was taken out of the detailed publication altogether. The 2021 report states it plainly: the detailed information “cannot be published in the report or dashboards for confidentiality reasons”. The money is inside the annual totals; it is not in the dashboard, and so it is not in this file.

The report's own figures barely move. We read the same series out of all eleven editions of the report, 2015 through 2025: a year, once published, is restated the same way edition after edition — 2015 appears in all eleven as the identical $17,575 million. The only exception is small and unexplained: the 2020 and 2021 editions put 2016–2019 between $2m and $10m higher than every other edition, before and after. That is 0.01% to 0.06% of those years, and we do not know what caused it.

How this was made. The analysis behind this dataset was done in Claude Code with a set of Agentic Skills written for investigative work: deterministic tools that seal a corpus, keep a ledger of candidate findings, assemble the records behind each one, and recheck every derived figure against its source tables. Nine of them are published under an MIT licence, as a submission to the Agentic Investigation Challenge. They were built for a different corpus and are still being developed; this one is among the datasets they are tested against.
